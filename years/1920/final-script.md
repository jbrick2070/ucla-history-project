# 1920: A Green Tail and the Wrong Collar

## Final explainer script

- **Scope:** January 1–December 31, 1920 only
- **Target length:** 5–6 minutes
- **Voice:** One narrator
- **Audio:** Narration only—no music, sound effects, dialogue, or character voices
- **Visual language:** Warm paper, loose hand-drawn ink, handwritten marginal notes, dry visual humor, and restrained UCLA blue-and-gold washes. Keep archival pages legible. Label every reconstructed scene **Illustration**; never make an illustration look like newly discovered archival evidence.

Only the paragraphs under **Narrator voiceover** are spoken. Visual directions, descriptions, and citations are production guidance.

---

## Scene 1 — The dog with the obsolete paperwork

### Narrator voiceover

On December 3, 1920, the *Cub Californian* printed a secret about Rags, the Southern Branch campus dog. His Christmas stocking, the paper promised, would contain a new collar engraved “The University of California.” The old collar still said “Normal School.”

The archive confirms the plan, not whether the present ever arrived. But the mistake was perfect. In 1920 the whole Southern Branch was a little like Rags: officially part of a university, while still wearing pieces of its former identity. The students spent the year trying to make the new name mean something.

### Suggested visual

- Open on the December 3 front page. Push slowly into “SURPRISE ON WAY FOR MASCOT; DON’T TELL RAGS.”
- Highlight “The University of California” in blue and “Normal School” in gold.
- Cut to a clearly labeled **Illustration**: a shaggy dog viewed from behind, wearing an old collar while a blank gift tag waits beside him.

### Viewer-visible description

A genuine 1920 newspaper page reveals that Rags still wore a collar bearing the former school’s name. Handwritten arrows turn the collar into a small institutional identity crisis.

### Source and fact citation

- *Cub Californian*, December 3, 1920, p. 1, “Surprise on Way for Mascot; Don’t Tell Rags”: [page viewer](https://archive.org/details/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n436/mode/1up) · [direct scan](https://archive.org/download/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n436_w1200.jpg). Contemporary primary source; UCLA Library microfilm via Internet Archive.

## Scene 2 — January turns green

### Narrator voiceover

Rewind to January 13. Frosh Color Day turned the campus into what the yearbook called an “emerald city.” Green ties, socks, ribbons, and improvised costumes moved through the halls. Rags “flaunted a royal green tail.”

Then came the freshman band: trombone, trap drum, cymbals, bass drum, and a student whose vest was said to make as much noise as the instruments. The yearbook praised its “classical strains,” then added that “the discord continued most soulfully.” This was school spirit assembled from whatever could be worn, painted, struck, or blown into.

### Suggested visual

- Show the “Frosh Color Day” yearbook page and its real band photograph.
- Add restrained green wash only to details named by the sources; do not recolor the archival photograph itself.
- Briefly show the January 16 newspaper’s list of instruments, with small ink doodles in the margin.

### Viewer-visible description

The archival yearbook pairs a photograph of the makeshift freshman band with a report about an “emerald city,” Rags’s green tail, and soulful discord.

### Sources and fact citations

- *Southern Campus* 1920, printed p. 28, “Frosh Color Day”: [page viewer](https://archive.org/details/southerncampu1920univ/page/n27/mode/1up) · [direct scan](https://archive.org/download/southerncampu1920univ/page/n27_w1200.jpg). Contemporary yearbook source.

## Scene 3 — A class with a yell leader

### Narrator voiceover

The comedy shared the campus with a harder postwar reality. Men injured during military service were studying through a federal vocational program. The 1920 yearbook counted 175 Federal students and said that on January 30 they met to organize themselves “into a strong force.”

They elected a president, vice-president, secretary, treasurer, and sergeant-at-arms. Then they elected a yell leader. That last office says something the formal titles do not. These men were not only enrolled in a vocational-rehabilitation program. They were trying to take part in university life.

### Suggested visual

- Reveal the full Federal Class yearbook page, then move from the text to the large group photograph.
- Handwritten marginal list: “president / treasurer / sergeant-at-arms / yell leader.” Circle the final office.
- Do not invent individual identities for people in the group photograph.

### Viewer-visible description

A real 1920 group portrait sits beneath the yearbook’s account of 175 Federal students organizing themselves and electing officers—including a yell leader.

### Source and fact citation

- *Southern Campus* 1920, printed p. 156, “The Federal Class”: [page viewer](https://archive.org/details/southerncampu1920univ/page/n155/mode/1up) · [direct scan](https://archive.org/download/southerncampu1920univ/page/n155_w1200.jpg). Contemporary yearbook source.

## Scene 4 — Fund the university; ride the tiny train

### Narrator voiceover

That fall, the Southern Branch needed Californians to approve Amendment 12, a statewide measure intended to provide continuing university income. The campus response was political education by way of a circus.

The surviving circus paper advertised hot dogs, vaudeville, peanuts, a fat-lady attraction, a slide for life, and a miniature train. A near-contemporary campus calendar added that the Psychology Department “screams wildly through a ride on Kenny’s ‘Scenic Railway.’” Across the joke newspaper ran the serious commands “KEEP EDUCATION FREE” and “STAMP YOUR BALLOT YES ON AMENDMENT 12.”

The campus was learning that a public university had to explain itself to the public. Apparently it could do so with constitutional finance, pink lemonade, and a parody-paper joke about a small railway accident.

### Suggested visual

- Use both pages of the October 1 *Scrub Californian*, beginning with the enormous word “PEANUTS.”
- Pan across the circus listings, vaudeville program, Amendment 12 slogans, and verse about the miniature train tipping into a ditch.
- Show the retrospective calendar entry only long enough to establish the scenic-railway line; label it “yearbook account of a 1920 event.”

### Viewer-visible description

A parody newspaper made for the campus circus mixes jokes, attractions, and campaign slogans. Marginal blue-and-gold arrows connect a miniature railway to a statewide university-funding vote.

### Sources and fact citations

- *Scrub Californian*, October 1, 1920, pp. 1–2, circus issue: [front page](https://archive.org/details/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n398/mode/1up) · [second page](https://archive.org/details/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n399/mode/1up). Contemporary primary source.

## Scene 5 — Who gets a vote?

### Narrator voiceover

The Federal students’ January organization led to a more basic question: did they fully count in student government?

On October 20, the student body amended its constitution. The relevant amendment passed 429 to 27. The next morning, some two hundred Federal men formed “a huge serpentine,” the *Cub Californian* reported on October 22. They crossed the campus, entered assembly, curled onto the stage, and answered the student body’s cheer with one of their own. The paper concluded: “Millspaugh hall has rarely seen such a demonstration of good fellowship.”

The scene was exuberant, but the issue beneath it was not decorative. Campus belonging had required an election.

### Suggested visual

- Start on the October 22 headline: “SPECIAL ELECTION GIVES FEDERALS VOTING PRIVILEGE.”
- Animate the printed numbers 429 and 27 into a simple hand-drawn tally.
- Use a clearly labeled **Illustration** for the serpentine: an overhead ink line winding across a simplified campus plan and into Millspaugh Hall.

### Viewer-visible description

The contemporary newspaper supplies the election result and describes roughly two hundred Federal men winding across campus after receiving student-body voting privileges.

### Source and fact citation

- *Cub Californian*, October 22, 1920, p. 1, “Special Election Gives Federals Voting Privilege”: [page viewer](https://archive.org/details/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n412/mode/1up) · [direct scan](https://archive.org/download/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n412_w1200.jpg). Contemporary primary source.

## Scene 6 — Politics by electric sign and airplane

### Narrator voiceover

Meanwhile, Amendment 12 campaigning escaped the circus tent. Engineers put an electric campaign sign on the gym. The campus calendar recorded pounds of literature dropped from airplanes—“not all at once,” it helpfully clarified. Late in October, a downtown parade put a dilapidated stagecoach, representing the university “as it is today,” ahead of a polished limousine representing what the amendment might make possible. On November 2, classes were dismissed while students campaigned.

The measure lost statewide by 4,640 votes. The tiny margin made the absurd machinery of the campaign feel less absurd. A sign, a leaflet, a parade, or a miniature train might actually have mattered.

### Suggested visual

- Show the October 19, October 25, and November 2 calendar entries on the same archival page.
- Build a clearly labeled **Illustration** of paper leaflets drifting over a line-drawn Vermont Avenue campus; each leaflet carries a visible “12.”
- Pair the yearbook’s real campaign photographs with a clearly labeled **Illustration** of the stagecoach-and-limousine parade.
- End on the yearbook’s December 6 entry recording the 4,640-vote margin.

### Viewer-visible description

Near-contemporary sources record an electric sign, campaign literature dropped from airplanes, a stagecoach-and-limousine parade, dismissed classes, and the final 4,640-vote loss.

## Scene 7 — Rags joins the Y

### Narrator voiceover

On November 24, the newspaper reported another membership decision. Rags had joined the campus YMCA.

The fee was one dollar. Because the paper declared him not yet twenty-one, his guardian, Miss Hallam, handled the transaction. Then, it said, “Rags signed the card with his faithful paw” and wore the organization’s red triangle around his neck.

This was obviously a campus joke. It was also another little ceremony of inclusion. In 1920, even the dog acquired membership papers before he acquired the correct collar.

### Suggested visual

- Show the November 24 headline and article beside the page’s real photograph of the first varsity football team.
- Add a clearly labeled **Illustration** of a paw print on a membership card and a red triangle hanging from a collar.
- Do not portray the paw signature as a surviving artifact; it is described in the newspaper, not reproduced there.

### Viewer-visible description

A 1920 front page reports that Rags paid a dollar to join the YMCA, with Miss Hallam conducting the business and the dog supposedly signing by paw.

### Source and fact citation

- *Cub Californian*, November 24, 1920, p. 1, “Famed Personage Added to Y.M.C.A. Membership Roll”: [page viewer](https://archive.org/details/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n432/mode/1up) · [direct scan](https://archive.org/download/ucladailybruin01losa/page/n432_w1200.jpg). Contemporary primary source.

## Scene 8 — Twenty-three minutes and a flunkless year

### Narrator voiceover

The year ended with paperwork and comedy sharing the same front page.

On December 17, the newspaper summoned all male students to a compulsory ROTC enrollment assembly and instructed each one to bring a fountain pen. At the Southern Branch, even military training arrived first as paperwork.

On December 24, the paper reported that the Federal Class had distributed about fourteen thousand dollars in pay checks among roughly 250 men in exactly twenty-three minutes. The class had helped devise and approve the system. Nearby, another article pretended that Rags had bought and decorated the cafeteria Christmas tree, polishing apples and oranges by rolling them across the floor. It wished everyone a “Flunkless New Year.”

That is 1920 at the Southern Branch: a green tail, a veterans’ organization with a yell leader, a circus for constitutional finance, an election over who belonged, campaign leaflets falling from airplanes, twenty-three minutes of highly efficient paperwork, and one dog trapped between two institutional names.

The new collar may or may not have arrived. The new identity was already being made—one vote, joke, form, and improvised tradition at a time.

### Suggested visual

- Split the December 24 front page between “DISTRIBUTION PLAN BREAKS ALL RECORDS” and “RAGS IN GUISE OF SANTA GIVES CHRISTMAS TREE.”
- Briefly show the December 17 ROTC headline and highlight the instruction to bring a fountain pen.
- Let hand-drawn checks stack into a neat pile while illustrated apples roll untidily across the margin.
- Return to the December 3 collar story. End on the two phrases “Normal School” and “The University of California.”
- Final card: “Independent historical explainer; not an official UCLA production.”

### Viewer-visible description

A December ROTC notice begins with a fountain pen; one week later, a Christmas Eve newspaper page places an efficient Federal Class pay-check operation beside a comic Rags story. The closing image returns to the collar whose engraved name no longer matched the institution.

### Source and fact citation

Contemporary primary source.

## Production guardrails

- Keep every narrated event inside calendar year 1920.
- Call the institution the **Southern Branch** in narration; do not place it in Westwood or show later campus landmarks.
- Do not call Rags a stray, a lost dog, or the first official mascot. The 1920 sources used here establish only that he was a beloved campus dog treated as a mascot.
- Do not say the new collar was delivered. The newspaper documents only the planned gift.
- Keep the Federal-student counts tied to their dates and sources: 175 in the 1920 yearbook account; roughly 200 in the October serpentine; roughly 250 in the December pay-check distribution.
- Present the Rags Christmas-tree article as newspaper humor, not literal reportage.
- Present every reconstructed image as **Illustration**. Never fabricate archival photographs, forms, signatures, or individual identities.
- Credit the *Cub Californian* reel to UCLA Library microfilm via Internet Archive, and the yearbooks to UCLA Library via Internet Archive. The cited 1920 and 1921 publications are in the U.S. public domain; retain repository credits.
