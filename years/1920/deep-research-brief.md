# UCLA 1920 — Deep-Research Brief

## Assignment

Create a source-ready research report about the Southern Branch of the University of California during **calendar year 1920 only: January 1–December 31, 1920**.

Use the attached `PROJECT-BIBLE.md` for research and citation standards. Include only events that occurred during 1920. A source published later may be used only when it provides evidence about a specifically dated 1920 event, and it must be labeled retrospective.

The report will later support a 4–6 minute NotebookLM explainer, but **do not write the finished video script yet**. First build the strongest possible evidence packet.

## Central research challenge

Determine what 1920 actually felt like at the still-new Southern Branch by finding the overlooked details that conventional institutional histories leave out.

Do not produce a greatest-achievements survey or force the evidence into a triumphal story. Look for revealing small material: a room borrowed for an unexpected purpose, a rule students resisted, a newspaper joke, a strange advertisement, a complaint, a commute, an awkward meeting, a lopsided contest, a lost or misplaced object, an administrative workaround, an improbable club, or an ordinary person briefly visible in the record.

The goal is to discover **quirky, little-known, writing-worthy 1920 stories that give us a genuinely new perspective on the institution**. Remain open to a better, stranger, or less familiar narrative discovered in the sources.

## Discovery requirement

Find at least **20 candidate microstories** before selecting the strongest material. For each candidate, provide:

- a one-sentence description;
- the exact 1920 date or best-supported date range;
- the primary source and direct page link;
- what makes it surprising or revealing;
- whether it is fully verified, plausible but incomplete, disputed, or folklore;
- what additional evidence would confirm it.

Then rank the ten strongest candidates by **freshness, human interest, visual potential, historical significance, and strength of evidence**. Famous events may appear only if the sources reveal an unfamiliar angle.

## Questions to investigate

### Institution and physical campus

- What changed institutionally during 1920 in ways that produced an unusual human-scale story?
- What programs, courses, departments, offices, or administrative roles produced odd arrangements, conflicts, or improvisations?
- How were inherited Normal School buildings and rooms actually being used?
- Can maps, floor plans, directories, photographs, or descriptions reconstruct a believable room-by-room campus route?
- What did students encounter when they arrived by streetcar or on foot?

### Students and daily life

- Who attended in 1920: ages, gender balance, prior schooling, teachers in training, degree students, veterans, commuters, and other identifiable groups?
- What evidence survives about housing approval, meals, clothing, expenses, schedules, discipline, health, clubs, courtship, work, and commuting?
- Which rules or ordinary inconveniences reveal the institution most clearly?
- Which students, employees, or faculty members are visible as people rather than names on an organizational chart?

### Veterans and federal rehabilitation

- Establish the exact 1920 chronology of disabled ex-service students and federal vocational rehabilitation at the Branch.
- Verify when a formal student organization was created, what it was called at the time, who participated, and how contemporary sources described it.
- Investigate enrollment totals carefully. Distinguish a point-in-time count, academic-year total, retrospective estimate, and later membership claim.
- Examine the campaign for student-body participation or voting rights. Reconcile newspaper chronology with later yearbook retellings rather than silently blending them.
- Look beyond organization-building for individual experiences, courses, facilities, tensions, and everyday campus interactions.

### Student government, media, and culture

- Trace student-government elections, constitutional changes, meetings, demonstrations, rallies, or disputes during 1920.
- Read the student newspaper issue by issue for recurring jokes, advertisements, corrections, letters, gossip, editorial campaigns, and small notices.
- Investigate the January 1920 freshman-band story and verify its exact date, names, wording, and context.
- Determine what happened with the campus mascot and other traditions during 1920 without repeating unsupported folklore.
- Identify performances, publications, ceremonies, clubs, and informal traditions that existed specifically in 1920.

### Athletics

- Reconstruct the 1920 athletic calendar from contemporary evidence.
- Identify opponents, venues, scores, transportation, eligibility, coaching, equipment, attendance, and how students wrote about victories or defeats.
- Explain why a new university branch played the opponents it did, using contemporary Los Angeles sports coverage where possible.
- Do not import an athletic event from any other year.

### Los Angeles and the outside world

- Connect the Branch to 1920 Los Angeles through streetcars, neighborhoods, public schools, newspapers, civic organizations, employment, and postwar change.
- Determine how teacher-training continuity shaped the new university branch.
- Investigate how demobilization, disability, federal rehabilitation policy, public education, gender expectations, or local population growth touched daily campus life.
- Avoid generic national context unless a source demonstrates a direct Southern Branch connection.

## Leads that must be checked, not assumed

Use these as search leads. Verify each against the original page before reporting it:

- A January 1920 meeting associated with organizing disabled ex-service students.
- A January 1920 freshman-band or rally account in the student newspaper.
- A fall 1920 campaign concerning federal students and student-body voting privileges.
- A reported October election result and a subsequent serpentine or campus demonstration.
- Conflicting chronology between a contemporary student-newspaper account and a later yearbook summary.
- Enrollment or membership numbers that may describe different dates or populations.

If one of these leads proves incorrect, misdated, exaggerated, or less interesting than another discovery, say so plainly and follow the stronger evidence.

## Required source hierarchy

1. Contemporary 1920 student newspapers and other period newspapers.
2. Official University catalogs, announcements, Regents records, minutes, directories, and reports.
3. Contemporary photographs, maps, transit material, programs, advertisements, and ephemera.
4. Near-contemporary yearbooks and institutional records, with the year and retrospective nature identified.
5. Later scholarly or institutional histories for orientation and leads—not as substitutes for primary evidence.

Search at minimum:

- Internet Archive runs of the *Cub Californian* and early UCLA student newspaper.
- Contemporary and near-contemporary *Southern Campus* yearbooks, used only for evidence about events that occurred in 1920.
- University of California Southern Branch catalogs and announcements covering 1920.
- UCLA Library Digital Collections and University Archives finding aids.
- UC Regents records and official reports.
- California Digital Newspaper Collection and accessible Los Angeles newspapers.
- Los Angeles city directories, maps, photographs, streetcar maps, and timetables.

## Evidence rules

- Every material claim needs a citation with title, publication or creator, exact date, page or image locator, repository, and direct landing-page URL.
- Prefer stable item or page URLs over search-result links.
- Provide short exact quotations, preserving original spelling and punctuation. Never put quotation marks around a paraphrase.
- Label every source as contemporary primary, near-contemporary, later retrospective, or secondary.
- Assign each material claim a confidence level: High, Medium, Low, or Disputed.
- Do not silently resolve conflicting dates, names, scores, counts, or sequences.
- Distinguish event date, publication date, academic year, calendar year, and retrospective reporting date.
- Record unsuccessful searches when they close an important lead.
- Do not invent facts, motives, dialogue, scenes, quotations, dates, or causal connections.

## Required report structure

Return one clean Markdown document with these sections:

1. **Executive findings** — the most important verified discoveries and the unexpected perspective they provide on 1920.
2. **Strict 1920 timeline** — dated events from January 1 through December 31 only.
3. **Twenty candidate microstories** — with verification status and primary-source links.
4. **Ranked top ten** — explaining the new perspective offered by each story.
5. **Evidence matrix** — claim, date, source, locator, URL, source tier, confidence, and notes.
6. **Exact quote bank** — short verbatim quotations with complete citations and explanations of possible use.
7. **Contradiction and folklore audit** — competing accounts, later embellishments, ambiguous language, and unresolved discrepancies.
8. **Human-scale discoveries** — daily routines, spaces, objects, rules, jokes, inconveniences, and overlooked people.
9. **Three unconventional narrative spines** — each grounded in evidence, with a beginning, development, ending, strongest sources, and weaknesses.
10. **Image and video-source manifest** — at least 12 useful archival candidates with date, creator, landing-page URL, direct image URL when available, rights status, required credit, and what the asset actually depicts.
11. **Illustration opportunities** — scenes that could be responsibly reconstructed, clearly labeled as illustrations, without fabricating archival photographs.
12. **Research gaps** — missing records, unresolved questions, and the next searches most likely to succeed.
13. **Excluded material** — interesting items rejected because they did not occur in 1920.
14. **Full bibliography** — separated into primary, near-contemporary, retrospective, and secondary sources.

## Visual and production constraints

- Favor archival pages, photographs, maps, directories, timetables, objects, and locations actually connected to 1920.
- Never present a generated reconstruction as archival evidence.
- Keep later Westwood iconography offstage unless used briefly and explicitly for orientation.
- Describe possible illustration style through formal traits: hand-drawn linework, restrained UCLA blue-and-gold accents, paper texture, marginal annotations, dry visual humor, and book-page composition.
- Do not name or imitate a particular artist.
- The eventual production will use one narrator, with visual notes separated from narration and no music, sound effects, dramatized dialogue, or character voices.

## Final quality-control checklist

Before delivering the report, confirm that:

- every narrated event occurred in calendar year 1920;
- every quotation was checked against the displayed source page;
- every image link opens to the claimed object;
- contemporary and retrospective evidence are clearly distinguished;
- contradictions remain visible;
- every event outside 1920 has been excluded;
- the report contains enough source detail for another researcher to reproduce the work;
- the narrative possibilities emerge from evidence rather than from institutional mythology.
